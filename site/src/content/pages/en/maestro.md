## Afternoons after secondary school

Felipe Linares Vargas remembers that, coming home from secondary school, he would arrive at the workshop and sit beside his father, Felipe Linares Mendoza, in the same spot where he works today. Among conversation, paper and paint he was given small tasks: "help me whiten this, help me decorate that." He devoted himself, he says, "to watching and doing it."

Over the years those afternoons ended up defining his path. He completed a degree in programming, but on finishing it he knew his place was not in an office.

> "I was more drawn to being here with my dad, because here I expressed my creativity."
>
> Felipe Linares Vargas, video testimony

## An inherited name, a craft of his own

He is the son of Felipe Linares Mendoza and the grandson of Pedro Linares López, the cartonero who dreamed of the alebrijes in 1936 and gave them their name. He carries his father's first name and the surname of a family that Mexico City has identified with paper and cardboard for more than a century. But the name does not explain the work. The hand does.

In the Linares family each member keeps a workshop of their own and makes alebrijes in their own style. His brother Leonardo sums it up: "here, the forbidden thing was to forbid." You learn by watching your father and grandfather, imitating, making mistakes and starting again. Felipe belongs to that generation, the fifth by the family's own count, which grew up among Judas figures for Holy Week, skulls for November and alebrijes all year round.

## Barbas: a story turned alebrije

One day a little rooster walked into the Linares home, climbed the stairs and stayed. His father named it Barbas. It became a much-loved pet that followed him everywhere.

Some time later Felipe wanted to turn that memory into an alebrije: "I'm going to make a little rooster like Barbas." While he wondered what head to give it, his father suggested a lion's, "so it looks strong and imposing." Barbas ended up as a fantastic creature born of affection and of the collaboration between father and son. "My dad and I are a little more connected when it comes to working together," Felipe says.

## What the work shows

The pieces gathered on this site show a recognizable repertoire: alebrijes that combine felines, birds, reptiles and amphibians in a single body; dressed and animated skeletons; and compositions in which a skeleton rides a fantastic creature. What distinguishes them lies on the surface: jaguar spots, scales, wing veins, fur and dots of different sizes, resolved by brush with a precision that turns the smooth volume of paper into texture.

That attention to detail is the contribution the family credits to Felipe Linares Mendoza: working the pieces as sculptures, with more movement and more height, and painting with a skill that, as Leonardo says, "I still can't surpass." In his son's work that lesson is recognizable at once.

> "Cartonería is very malleable, it can be handled in many ways; there is no limit. The limit is the one you set yourself. We want to follow the line of my grandfather and my father, but with our own creativity and different forms."
>
> Felipe Linares Vargas, video testimony

## Work in collections and exhibitions

The Museo Nacional de Culturas Populares in Mexico City holds "Tzompantli" (2010) in its collection, a work in molded, modeled and polychromed cardboard measuring 122 × 104 × 20 centimeters, included in the digital story "The Festival of Death in Mexico" that the museum published on Google Arts & Culture.

In 2026, a rooster alebrije of his is part of "Alebrijes: A Linares Family Tradition", the traveling exhibition of the Museum of International Folk Art in Santa Fe, New Mexico, which opened on May 5, 2026 at the Consulate of Mexico in Albuquerque and will tour the American Southwest through 2028.
