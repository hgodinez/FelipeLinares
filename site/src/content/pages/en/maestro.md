## An inherited name, a craft of his own

Felipe Linares Vargas is the son of Felipe Linares Mendoza and the grandson of Pedro Linares López, the cartonero who dreamed of the alebrijes in 1936 and gave them their name. He carries his father's first name and the surname of a family that Mexico City has identified with paper and cardboard for more than a century. But the name does not explain the work. The hand does.

In the Linares family each member keeps a workshop of their own. His brother Leonardo Linares Vargas has described it in interviews: you learn by helping your father, your grandfather and your uncles, and then each one works independently. Felipe Linares Vargas belongs to that generation, the fifth by the family's own count, which grew up among Judas figures for Holy Week, skulls for November and alebrijes all year round.

## What the work shows

The pieces gathered on this site show a recognizable repertoire: alebrijes that combine felines, birds, reptiles and amphibians in a single body; dressed and animated skeletons; and compositions in which a skeleton rides a fantastic creature. What distinguishes them lies on the surface. Jaguar spots, scales, wing veins, fur and dots of different sizes are resolved by brush with a precision that turns the smooth volume of paper into texture.

That attention to painted detail is, as Leonardo Linares has explained, the contribution Felipe Linares Mendoza made to the family: polishing pieces so they are worked as sculptures, and being meticulous with the paint. In his son's work that lesson is recognizable at once.

## Work in collections and exhibitions

The Museo Nacional de Culturas Populares in Mexico City holds "Tzompantli" (2010) in its collection, a work in molded, modeled and polychromed cardboard measuring 122 × 104 × 20 centimeters, included in the digital story "The Festival of Death in Mexico" that the museum published on Google Arts & Culture.

In 2026, a rooster alebrije of his is part of "Alebrijes: A Linares Family Tradition", the traveling exhibition of the Museum of International Folk Art in Santa Fe, New Mexico, which opened on May 5, 2026 at the Consulate of Mexico in Albuquerque and will tour the American Southwest through 2028. The show brings together works by three generations of the family.

## What remains to be told

This site was built from verifiable public sources: museum records, institutional statements, interviews and press. There is much that only Felipe himself can tell, when he modeled his first piece, what he learned from his grandfather, which animals he prefers to combine, and that material will be added in his own voice, not invented by anyone else. The sections marked "to be confirmed" are waiting for exactly that.
