The Linares family tells that it all began when don Pedro fell gravely ill. A gastric ulcer put him in bed and, as the family remembers, even into a coma. Between dreams and delirium he saw extraordinary creatures: beings that seemed to gather parts of different animals ("like a monkey's face with a frog's body," he would say), figures as unsettling as they were fascinating, shouting an unknown word over and over: *alebrijes!*

When he recovered, don Pedro told his children: "I'm going to make some figures that appeared to me when I was sick." With paper, cardboard, paste and his imagination, those creatures began to exist outside his dreams. Over time he consolidated what an alebrije is: a fantastic figure that gathers parts of several different animals.

## Here, the forbidden thing was to forbid

In the Linares workshop the craft did not begin with lessons or instructions. It began as play. The children watched their father and grandfather at work; they picked up newspaper, prepared paste and tried to reproduce the figures they saw appearing between their hands. There was no written method: you learned by watching, trying, failing and starting again.

> "Here, the forbidden thing was to forbid."
>
> Leonardo Linares Vargas

That freedom was also a way of teaching. Each generation discovered, in its own time, how to turn everyday materials into creatures that existed nowhere else.

## The son who learned by watching

Felipe Linares Mendoza, don Pedro's son, tells that he saw his father receive orders for alebrijes and said to himself, "I'll make one and see how it comes out." His first alebrije was a figure like an armadillo with a skull on top. Don Pedro went so far as to tell him, "you're already beating me at making alebrijes."

Over the years he was invited to teach courses in San Antonio, Texas, and later in Los Angeles, San Diego and other places; he gave cartonería demonstrations at Disneyland. That is how, he says, "we have kept this tradition alive."

## The craft inherited through the hands

Felipe Linares Vargas remembers the afternoons after secondary school, when he arrived at the workshop and sat beside his father. Among conversation, paper and paint he received small tasks: whitening a figure, decorating a piece, watching how a detail was resolved. Over the years those afternoons defined his path. Although he studied programming, he found that his place was in the workshop.

> "I was more drawn to being here with my dad, because here I expressed my creativity."
>
> Felipe Linares Vargas

Knowledge was not passed on only through words. It was in the way a hand moved, in the patience to build a figure and in the care with which each brushstroke was applied.

## A tradition that keeps imagining

The essential materials remain: newspaper, cardboard, flour, water and paint. But the possibilities keep growing. In the hands of Felipe and Leonardo, alebrijes take on new forms, movements, heights, textures and details. Each piece keeps the knowledge of those who came before, but also expresses the imagination of whoever makes it.

It is not about repeating the figures of the past exactly. It is about keeping the craft alive, experimenting with its possibilities and finding new ways of giving life to the impossible. Because in the Linares family tradition is not a form that must stay still: it is an art that keeps transforming, generation after generation.
