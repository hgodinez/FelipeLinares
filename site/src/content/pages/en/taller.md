sections:
  - heading: Imagine
    text: Every alebrije begins as a combination. A feline with antlers, a frog with a beak, a tiger with wings and a serpent's tail. The tradition Pedro Linares López began consists of gathering parts of different animals into one body, and deciding what proportion belongs to each.
  - heading: The armature
    text: Traditional Mexico City cartonería rises over a light framework of wire, reed or cardboard that sets the figure's posture and supports the parts that project outward, such as wings, antlers and tails.
  - heading: Paper and paste
    text: Layers of paper and cardboard are applied over the armature with engrudo, a paste of flour and water. Each layer dries before the next; the piece gains rigidity without losing lightness.
  - heading: Model
    text: With hands and simple tools the volumes are refined (the muzzle, the eyes, the claws) and the joins corrected until the figure has a continuous surface, ready to take color.
  - heading: Dry
    text: The piece dries in the air, sometimes for days. This waiting time decides the durability of the object; a poorly dried base warps under paint.
  - heading: Paint
    text: First a base coat over the whole surface. Then the large fields of color that distinguish each part of the body.
  - heading: Detail
    text: Spots, scales, veins, fur and dots of different sizes are painted by brush, one by one. It is the longest stage and the one that gives each piece its character.
  - heading: Finish
    text: A final varnish or protective coat fixes the color and gives the surface its sheen. The piece is ready to leave the workshop.
    note: The photographs on this page were taken in the family workshop; they show pieces at different stages of the process. Felipe Linares Vargas's own workshop will be documented with dedicated photography and video as soon as they are available.

The Linares family works in the Merced Balbuena area, east of Mexico City's historic center, near the Sonora Market, where the houses are workshops at the same time. Each member keeps their own. On these tables, jars of paint, brushes, half-dried pieces and finished figures waiting for their owners all share the space.

The steps that follow describe the general process of traditional cartonería as documented by the sources consulted and as seen in the workshop photographs included on this site. The specifics of Felipe Linares Vargas's own method (drying times, types of paint, tools) will be added with his confirmation.
