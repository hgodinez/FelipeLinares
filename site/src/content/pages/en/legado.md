  - heading: Before the dream
    text: Cartonería is an old craft in Mexico City. Cartoneros worked to the rhythm of the festive calendar (Judas figures for Holy Week, skulls for the Day of the Dead, piñatas for Christmas) and the Linares family practiced it before Pedro Linares López, whose father and grandfather worked in cardboard.
  - heading: 1936. Pedro Linares's dream
    text: "At thirty, Pedro Linares López fell gravely ill. Convalescing, he dreamed of a forest inhabited by colorful creatures (a donkey with wings, a rooster with horns, a lion with a dog's head) that repeated a meaningless word, \"alebrijes\". On recovering, he began to model them in paper and cardboard. He had created a new category within an old craft."
  - heading: Painters, documentaries and a national prize
    text: "Diego Rivera and Frida Kahlo bought figures from Pedro Linares; some are preserved in the Museo Anahuacalli. In 1975, Judith Bronowski's documentary \"Pedro Linares, artesano de cartón\" brought his work to an international public, and in 1990 he received the National Prize for Arts and Sciences in the Popular Arts and Traditions category. He died in January 1992."
  - heading: The sons' generation
    text: Enrique, Felipe and Miguel Linares learned the craft from their father and expanded its possibilities. Felipe Linares Mendoza specialized in animated skeletons and brought the polishing and painted detail of the pieces to a sculptural level; for the Day of the Dead in 1986 he made a work about the 1985 earthquake with more than fifty life-size skeletons. Miguel Linares keeps a workshop in La Merced.
  - heading: The grandchildren, each in their own workshop
    text: Felipe, Leonardo and David Linares Vargas, sons of Felipe Linares Mendoza, and Ricardo Linares García work today in independent workshops. Leonardo has been the family's public voice in the declaration of the alebrijes as cultural heritage of Mexico City (2019); Felipe Linares Vargas has work in the Museo Nacional de Culturas Populares and in the Museum of International Folk Art's traveling exhibition (2026–2028). A sixth generation, Gerardo Linares Gómez, Pedro's great-grandson, already takes part in public events.
  - heading: The alebrije as heritage
    text: Since 2007, the Museo de Arte Popular has organized a parade of monumental alebrijes along Paseo de la Reforma every October. In October 2019, the Government of Mexico City declared the alebrijes cultural heritage of the city; the decree was published in the Official Gazette on December 30 of that year. Museums such as the Fowler Museum (UCLA), the Museo Nacional de Culturas Populares and the Museum of International Folk Art have devoted exhibitions and catalogues to the family.

## Cartonería and wood: two distinct traditions

The word "alebrije" is used today for two different things, and it is worth telling them apart.

**Cartonería alebrijes** were born in Mexico City, in the workshop of Pedro Linares López, from 1936 onward. They are built with paper, cardboard and paste over a light armature, modeled by hand and painted with brushes. They are hollow, light, and can reach a large scale; they belong to the urban tradition of Judas figures, skulls and piñatas.

**Oaxacan carvings** are figures of fantastic animals sculpted in copal wood, mainly in the villages of San Antonio Arrazola and San Martín Tilcajete in the Central Valleys. That region already had a tradition of carving animals in wood; from the 1960s, artisans such as Manuel Jiménez began painting them in vivid colors and, over time, the market applied the name "alebrijes" to them as well.

Both traditions are valuable and both are Mexican. But the name, the concept and the original technique (the dream, the paper and the cardboard) belong to Mexico City and to the Linares family. This page documents that history with verifiable sources; whatever is not documented is marked as pending confirmation.
